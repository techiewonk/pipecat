# Open models now replicate ElevenLabs' cascade

ElevenLabs Agents is a first-party cascaded pipeline (Scribe realtime ASR, a proprietary turn-taking model plus VAD, a customer-chosen LLM, and Flash / v4 Turbo TTS) run by an in-house orchestrator, carried over a LiveKit server that ElevenLabs operates, hosted mainly on Google Cloud Kubernetes GPU clusters in three regions, and sold at a flat $0.08 per minute with LLM tokens and telephony passed through. Every layer of that stack now has a permissively licensed open equivalent, and the Pipecat checkout at `/home/user/pipecat` (version 1.11.0, BSD-2-Clause) already contains all of the non-model plumbing except a SIP stack, an SFU, guardrails and a deployment layer. Gepard, the Apache-2.0 streaming TTS from nineninesix.ai, plugs into Pipecat with no new code: its `gepard-vllm` server emulates the Cartesia API, so `CartesiaTTSService` pointed at the self-hosted URL is the whole integration. Published open cascades reach about 0.5 s voice-to-voice on one co-located GPU and 0.9 to 1.1 s at production concurrency, which matches what independent tests measure for ElevenLabs on real phone calls (1.0 to 1.4 s median). The gaps are elsewhere: open TTS still trails ElevenLabs' flagship on listener preference, Gepard officially covers only four languages and clones voices weakly, and nobody has published a fully open cascade running at thousands of concurrent calls. Cost favours self-hosting only above roughly 20,000 to 100,000 minutes a month once engineering time is counted, so the durable reasons to own the stack are control of turn-taking, data residency and a short subprocessor chain. The strongest things to build on top are vertical "close the call" products, deploy-anywhere packaging for regulated buyers, regional-language quality, and evals with caller-side latency reporting.

Two conventions apply throughout. Evidence labels on the ElevenLabs material are **Confirmed** (stated by ElevenLabs in docs, blog, SDK source, status page or job posts), **Third-party** (independent measurement or press) and **Inferred** (a deduction that ElevenLabs has not stated). Most latency, concurrency and quality numbers for models are vendor-measured; they are marked where that matters.

Three points decide how to copy this. First, LiveKit is ElevenLabs' media plane, not its orchestrator; ElevenLabs describes the orchestrator as purpose-built, and nothing indicates it uses the LiveKit Agents framework (inferred). Second, the published latency techniques are conventional and reproducible: start the LLM on stable partial transcripts, flush TTS at clause boundaries, race small self-hosted models for auxiliary steps, run guardrails and workflow conditions in parallel, match the codec to the transport, and serve from the nearest of three regions ([latency optimization post](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/voice-agent-latency-optimization), [orchestration engine post](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/unpacking-elevenagents-orchestration-engine)). Third, the structural advantage is co-location: ASR, turn model, TTS and orchestrator sit in one cluster, leaving the third-party LLM as the only cross-network hop inside a turn. ElevenLabs does not state this outright; it is an inference from the architecture, and it is the property a self-hosted stack can copy most directly.

Scale and reliability set the bar. Agents handle **more than 15 million conversations a week** ([tender announcement](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/tender-22bn)), yet the status page shows the stack strains at the edges: v4 Turbo generated audio slower than real time under EU load on 3 and 4 November 2026, SIP suffered two major disruptions in October, and Scribe v3 Live had an outage the day after launch ([status history](https://status.elevenlabs.io/history)). ElevenLabs' own latency budget is illustrative rather than measured (about 680 ms P50 and 1,560 ms P95), while independent phone-call tests put it at 1.04 to 1.42 s median ([latency optimization post](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/voice-agent-latency-optimization), [Cozzo](http://cozzo.io/benchmarks/phone-calls), [Openbenchmarks](https://openbenchmarks.com/voice-agent-latency/voice-agent-latency-comparison)).

The ASR and LLM layers are solved for English. Nemotron Speech Streaming beat Deepgram nova-3 and AssemblyAI on Pipecat's time-to-final benchmark, though at a higher semantic error rate (1.95% against 1.22% to 1.62%) ([Pipecat STT benchmark](https://www.pipecat.ai/benchmarks/speech-to-text)). On Daily's 30-turn voice benchmark, Gemma 4 31B and Qwen3.8 27B pass about 96% to 98% of turns, level with Claude Haiku 4.5 at 98.0%, and Qwen3.8 27B reached a **101 ms median TTFT on a local RTX 5090** against 649 ms on a hosted endpoint ([Micdrop LLM ranking](https://micdrop.dev/blog/top-llms-voice-agents), [Dograh open-weight LLMs](https://www.dograh.com/hub/blogs/open-weight-llms-voice-agents)). TTFT rises steeply with concurrency, so streams per GPU must stay well below throughput-optimal batch sizes; one vLLM test showed 2.1 s median TTFT at 50 concurrent requests on an H100 ([Winder.ai](https://winder.ai/vllm-vs-ollama-vs-sglang-llm-inference-comparison/)).

TTS is where open models still trail. On Artificial Analysis the best permissively licensed model, Ming-3.0-tts, sits at about 1187 Elo against 1339 for Eleven v4, and the best open-weight model overall (Fish Audio Drama 3, 1275) forbids hosted TTS services at any revenue ([gpuwerk](https://gpuwerk.com/blog/open-source-tts-models)). Open speech-to-speech is not an alternative yet: Moshi, PersonaPlex and NVIDIA's VoiceChat score 4% to 27% on Big Bench Audio against roughly 90% for hosted leaders, and most lack tool calling ([Dograh S2S](https://www.dograh.com/hub/blogs/open-source-speech-to-speech-models)).

## Gepard drops into Pipecat through the Cartesia service with no new code

Gepard is the TTS layer only. It is a roughly 556M-parameter autoregressive model (a 14-layer Qwen3.5 backbone with 32 audio heads and a stop head over NVIDIA's NanoCodec) released in mid-2026, with a robustness update, Gepard 1.1, in September ([Gepard 1.0 card](https://huggingface.co/nineninesix/gepard-1.0), [Gepard 1.1 card](https://huggingface.co/nineninesix/gepard-1.1)). Its design rule is that it must run on an unmodified vLLM engine, which is what gives it high concurrency per GPU ([nineninesix blog](https://www.nineninesix.ai/blog/why-gepard-runs-on-vllm)).

| Voice cloning | Zero-shot, but speaker similarity 0.585 against 0.79 to 0.87 for peers | [model card](https://huggingface.co/nineninesix/gepard-1.0) | Vendor-run benchmark; weak point confirmed by users |
| Accuracy | WER 0.036 on Seed-TTS-eval against 0.015 to 0.022 for leading peers; best predicted naturalness (NISQA 4.25) | [model card](https://huggingface.co/nineninesix/gepard-1.0) | Vendor-run |

The integration path is the existing Cartesia service. The local checkout contains no Gepard or nineninesix code, and none is needed: `CartesiaTTSService` at `/home/user/pipecat/src/pipecat/services/cartesia/tts.py` takes a `url` argument (default `wss://api.cartesia.ai/tts/websocket`) and `CartesiaHttpTTSService` takes `base_url`. The vendor README shows the same wiring ([gepard-vllm README](https://github.com/nineninesix-ai/gepard-vllm)). In this checkout the `voice_id=` and `model=` constructor arguments are deprecated in favour of `settings=`, so the call is:

```python
tts = CartesiaTTSService(
    api_key="ignored",
    url="ws://GEPARD_HOST:8000/tts/websocket",
    sample_rate=22050,
    settings=CartesiaTTSService.Settings(voice="<voice-uuid>", model="gepard-1.0"),
)
```

The recommended reference stack keeps every layer self-hostable and uses only adapters already in the repository.

| Layer | Recommended component | How it plugs into Pipecat (paths under `/home/user/pipecat/src/pipecat/`) | Runs on |
|---|---|---|---|
| Browser / app transport | Self-hosted LiveKit server (mirrors ElevenLabs), or SmallWebRTC for 1:1 calls without an SFU | `transports/livekit/`, `transports/smallwebrtc/` | CPU edge with stable public IP and UDP |
| Phone | SIP trunk into LiveKit SIP into a room; carrier media streams for a quick start | LiveKit transport; `serializers/twilio.py`, `telnyx.py`, `plivo.py`, `exotel.py` | CPU edge |
| Orchestrator | One `PipelineWorker` per call under a `WorkerRunner` | `pipeline/worker.py`, `workers/runner.py` | CPU pods |
| VAD + turn-taking | Silero VAD + Smart Turn v3.2, both bundled as ONNX | `audio/vad/silero.py`, `audio/turn/smart_turn/`, default in `turns/user_turn_strategies.py` | Same CPU pod |
| ASR | Nemotron Speech Streaming (English) or Nemotron 3.5 ASR Streaming (40 locales) | `services/nvidia/stt.py` over Riva gRPC to a local address | Shared GPU service |
| ASR, fully Apache route | Qwen3-ASR or Parakeet TDT per turn-detected segment | `services/openai/stt.py` with `base_url` to an OpenAI-compatible server | Shared GPU service |
| LLM | Gemma 4 31B or Qwen3.8 27B on vLLM or SGLang, reasoning off, prefix cache warm | `services/openai/llm.py` with `base_url` | Shared GPU service |
| TTS | Gepard via `gepard-vllm` (evaluate 1.1) | `services/cartesia/tts.py` with `url` override | Shared GPU service + Postgres |
| Knowledge | Postgres + pgvector, small self-hosted query rewriter with a hard timeout | Custom processor or tool; `services/mcp_service.py` for MCP tools | CPU + small GPU share |

| Recording | In-pipeline audio buffer, or LiveKit Egress | `processors/audio/audio_buffer_processor.py` | CPU |

The component choices follow the published open reference builds: NVIDIA ASR with Pipecat appears in four of them, including Daily's single-GPU build and NVIDIA's own blueprint ([Daily + NVIDIA](https://www.daily.co/blog/building-voice-agents-with-nvidia-open-models), [NVIDIA blueprint](https://build.nvidia.com/nvidia/nemotron-voice-agent)). The deployment shape, a CPU-only bot process per call calling separately autoscaled GPU services pinned to one availability zone, is the pattern Modal and an EKS reference both report, with about 1 ms per in-cluster hop ([Modal](https://modal.com/blog/low-latency-voice-bot), [WebRTC.ventures](https://webrtc.ventures/2026/09/self-hosted-voice-ai-pipeline-eks-karpenter-livekit)).

Four integration facts need hands-on checks before committing. Pipecat's Cartesia service requests word timestamps by default (`add_timestamps=True`) and uses them to keep the LLM context accurate after an interruption; whether the Gepard server returns them is undocumented. Pipecat sends whole sentences to TTS, so Gepard's prosody resets at each sentence boundary and its ability to start on partial text goes unused; effective first audio is LLM time-to-first-sentence plus about 50 ms ([gepard-vllm README](https://github.com/nineninesix-ai/gepard-vllm)). Gepard delivers audio in a few large chunks, which the vendor says leaves barge-in responsiveness "a separate question" ([nineninesix latency benchmark](https://www.nineninesix.ai/blog/tts-latency-benchmark-websocket-2026)). And the reference server ships with no authentication, open CORS, a debug endpoint and an unpinned vLLM dependency, so it needs a gateway, a pinned version and a Postgres instance added ([gepard-vllm README](https://github.com/nineninesix-ai/gepard-vllm)).

Pipecat itself leaves four things to build: there is no SIP endpoint or SFU in-tree, no dedicated guardrail or PII-redaction processor was found, there is no vector-store retriever, and deployment and autoscaling are out of scope. The checkout is also about four minor releases behind upstream (1.11.0 locally against 1.15.0 reported upstream on 29 October 2026) ([Sheerbit](https://sheerbit.com/pipecat-vs-livekit-agents/)).

Matching ElevenLabs as callers experience it does not require beating vendor headline claims of 400 to 600 ms, because no independent phone measurement shows any platform achieving them. It does require deliberate configuration: Cekura measured Pipecat at 3.15 s median on defaults, six times Daily's own figure for the same framework on one co-located GPU ([Cekura P99 analysis](https://www.cekura.ai/discover/p99-latency-voice-ai-agents)). Faster endpointing trades directly against cutting callers off; one benchmark found that none of 65+ stacks survived a 680 ms mid-sentence pause ([Codebridge](https://www.codebridge.tech/articles/voice-ai-phone-agents-latency-benchmark)). Latency and false-cutoff rate must therefore be set together, and measured from recordings at the caller's end.

Gepard changes the TTS arithmetic more than any other choice. TTS was 45% of one measured Vapi call ([GitHub discussion](https://github.com/baptistecristo/AI-agents-for-dumbphones/discussions/9)), and ElevenLabs Flash lists at $40 per million characters against Gepard's $5 hosted ([AssemblyAI TTS pricing guide](https://www.assemblyai.com/blog/tts-api-pricing), [nineninesix pricing](https://www.nineninesix.ai/pricing)). Self-hosting Gepard then makes sense for control, latency and data location rather than price, because a dedicated GPU already loses to cheap TTS APIs on pure cost until volume is high ([Arun Baby](https://www.arunbaby.com/speech-tech/0062-self-hosting-tts-production-economics/)). Idle capacity is the decisive variable: the same GPU costs about four times more per token at 25% utilisation than at full load ([Meetrix GPU costs](https://meetrix.io/blogs/gpu-costs-llm-inference-aws-vs-gcp/)). No source publishes measured concurrent calls per GPU for a complete open STT + LLM + TTS stack, so capacity must be load-tested.

Practitioner migrations confirm the direction and the caveat. Teams moving from Vapi or Retell to LiveKit or Pipecat report per-minute cost falling by half to three-quarters ($0.26 to $0.13, $0.10 to $0.025, $0.09 to $0.035), cite debuggability and turn-taking control as the trigger, and advise staying on a managed platform below about 20,000 minutes a month ([Piyush Gambhir](https://www.piyushgambhir.com/blogs/migrating-production-voice-agents-from-vapi-to-livekit), [Axionry](https://axionry.com/blog/migrate-from-retell-vapi-to-livekit), [Agent Tree](https://docs.agenttree.army/articles/voice-ai-self-hosted)). These figures exclude engineering cost, several authors sell migration services, and no account of a move in the reverse direction was found.

Three questions for the user would sharpen the design: which Gepard version and whether it will be self-hosted or the hosted API; the target languages and whether high-fidelity voice cloning is required; and the target GPU class and expected monthly minutes, which determine whether tier 2 or tier 3 economics apply.

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing of the problem. ElevenLabs' advantage is not a secret architecture: its cascade, its LiveKit transport and its latency techniques are all reproducible with Pipecat and open models, and its measured phone latency is within reach of a co-located open stack. What ElevenLabs owns that an open stack does not is voice quality at the top of the preference rankings, 90-language coverage, and an operations record at 15 million conversations a week. Gepard narrows the latency and cost gap at the TTS layer more than the quality or coverage gap, so it suits an English-first, latency-sensitive, cost-sensitive agent with fixed voices, and it should sit behind an interface that lets a second TTS serve other languages.

The practical sequence follows from the evidence. Stand up the reference stack with hosted Gepard and API models first, where the integration risk is a URL override; measure caller-side latency and false cutoffs with Pipecat's eval harness; then move models onto owned GPUs only when volume, residency or a quality requirement justifies the on-call burden. The product built on top should compete where hosted platforms are structurally weak (deployment inside the customer's boundary, depth in one vertical, regional languages, and honest published measurements) rather than on per-minute price, where the floor is already a cent.
